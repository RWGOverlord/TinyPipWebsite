Why is starting a task harder than doing it?

You know what needs doing. You might even want to do it. And yet the moment of beginning feels like pushing against a wall. Here’s what’s going on, and how to make that first moment smaller.

By the Tiny Pip team Published November 19, 2026 8 min read

The short answer: starting is harder than doing because almost everything that makes a task hard sits at the beginning: the unpleasant feelings about it, not knowing exactly what to do first, picturing the whole job at once, and having to stop what you’re doing now. Once you’re moving, most of that fades. The fix isn’t more willpower; it’s a first step small enough to take.

Key takeaways

  • Struggling to start is common and isn’t laziness. Research describes procrastination as a way of avoiding bad feelings in the moment.
  • Vague tasks are hard to start. “Do my taxes” isn’t an action; “find last year’s return” is.
  • The smaller and more physical the first step, the easier it is to begin.
  • If-then plans (“When I sit down after lunch, I’ll open the email”) are one of the best-studied ways to turn intentions into action.
  • Being kind to yourself about past procrastination makes the next start easier, not harder.

Why starting is the hardest part

Most tasks aren’t evenly difficult from beginning to end. The hard parts pile up at the start. Five things tend to meet there.

1. The bad feelings live at the beginning

Before you start, a task is mostly a feeling: dread about the bill, embarrassment about the late email, boredom at the thought of the laundry. Psychologist Piers Steel’s large review of procrastination research found that how unpleasant a task feels (“task aversiveness”) is one of the most consistent predictors of putting it off.1

Avoiding the task makes that feeling go away right now. Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl describe procrastination as choosing short-term mood repair over the thing you actually intended to do, with the cost passed on to your future self.2 Starting means walking straight into the feeling you’ve been avoiding. Doing the task, once you’re in it, often feels much more neutral.

2. The first step isn’t clear

Many things on a to-do list aren’t actions; they’re projects. “Taxes,” “sort out the car” and “clean the bedroom” each hide a dozen decisions. When your brain can’t see the first physical move, it reads the whole thing as hard and stalls. Uncertainty about where to begin is often mistaken for not wanting to do it.

3. You picture the whole task at once

Before you begin, you see the entire job: every drawer of the room, every page of the essay. While you’re doing it, you only face the next small piece. That’s why a task can feel enormous from the outside and manageable from the inside.

4. You have to stop doing something else

Starting always means leaving something: the couch, your phone, the show you’re halfway through. That switch has its own cost, separate from the task, and it’s easy to underestimate. Once you’ve switched, that cost is paid.

5. The reward is far away, the discomfort is now

The relief of a finished task arrives later. The discomfort of beginning arrives immediately. Steel’s research points to this gap, between a distant reward and a present cost, as a core reason people delay even things they care about.1 The further away the payoff, the weaker its pull on the present moment.

Put together, this explains a familiar experience: you avoid a task for days, finally start, and twenty minutes later think, that wasn’t so bad. It wasn’t that the task got easier. You got past the part where all the difficulty was stacked up.

Is it laziness?

Usually not. Laziness means not caring whether something gets done. People who struggle to start often care a lot, and that’s part of the problem: the tasks that matter most tend to carry the most stress, so they’re the ones that get avoided. Procrastination researchers describe it as a problem of handling feelings, not a lack of effort or character.2

This matters because calling yourself lazy makes starting harder. Shame is one more bad feeling to avoid, which feeds the same loop.

7 ways to make starting easier

Each of these makes the beginning of a task smaller, clearer or kinder, without asking for more willpower.

  1. Shrink the first step until it’s almost silly. Aim for something that takes about two minutes and that you could do even on a bad day. Not “clean the kitchen,” but “put the cups in the sink.” If you still don’t want to do it, it’s not small enough yet.
  2. Write the step as something you can see yourself doing. Start it with a verb and name the object: “open the bank website,” “find the landlord’s email,” “put the books on the desk.” If you can’t picture your hands doing it, break it down again.
  3. Make an if-then plan. Decide when and where the first step will happen: “When I finish lunch, I’ll open the email and read it.” These plans, called implementation intentions, have been tested in many studies, and a meta-analysis by Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran found they helped people follow through on goals.3 Later analyses suggest the effect is more modest than first estimated, but it’s a reliable, low-effort place to begin.
  4. Set a short timer, and mean it. Ten or twenty minutes gives the effort a clear end. It works best when stopping at the end is genuinely allowed. Often you’ll keep going; sometimes you won’t, and that still counts.
  5. Pick one thing, and put the list away. A long list makes every item compete for attention at once. Choose one task for now and look only at its first step.
  6. Count starting as the win. Finishing isn’t the only success. Starting is the hard part, so celebrate that: tell someone, tick a box, take a breath. Rewarding the start teaches your brain that beginning isn’t so bad.
  7. Leave a note for next time. When you stop, write one line about where you are and what comes next. Restarting is a lot easier when the first step is already decided.

And one more, which sounds soft but is backed by research: forgive yourself for the last time you put it off. In a study of university students, those who forgave themselves for procrastinating before a first exam procrastinated less before the next one.4 Being hard on yourself adds to the pile of bad feelings sitting at the start of the task.

When it might be more than procrastination

Everyone struggles to start sometimes. But if it’s constant, affects most areas of your life, or comes with persistent low mood, worry or exhaustion, it may be worth talking to a doctor or another qualified professional. Difficulty starting tasks is something many people with ADHD describe, and it can also show up with depression, anxiety or burnout. Tips like the ones above can help alongside proper support, but they aren’t a replacement for it.

Questions

Why is starting a task the hardest part?

Because the hardest parts of a task are concentrated at the beginning: the unpleasant feelings about it, not knowing exactly what to do first, picturing the whole job at once, and having to stop whatever you’re doing now. Once you’re moving, most of those ease, which is why doing often feels easier than starting.

Is not being able to start a task a sign of laziness?

Usually not. Research on procrastination describes it as a problem of managing feelings, not a lack of caring or effort. People often put off the things that matter most to them, precisely because those tasks carry the most stress.

What is the fastest way to start a task you’ve been avoiding?

Shrink the first step until it takes about two minutes and is a physical action you can picture, like “open the email” or “put the folder on the table.” Do only that step, and give yourself permission to stop afterward.

What is task initiation?

Task initiation is the ability to begin a task when it needs to be done, without a lot of delay. Difficulty with task initiation is common. Many people know exactly what to do and still find it hard to begin.

Does a timer help with starting?

For many people, yes. A short timer of 10 or 20 minutes puts a clear end on the effort, which makes beginning feel less open-ended. It works best when stopping at the end of the timer is genuinely allowed.

Is there an app that helps you start tasks?

Tiny Pip is one app built specifically around starting. It shows one task at a time, suggests a tiny first step, rewards you more for starting than for finishing, and has no streaks to lose. It’s a productivity and self-care app, not treatment for any condition.
